\begin{abstract}
Requirements Engineering (RE) is widely recognized as a critical phase in the software development lifecycle, yet it remains profoundly vulnerable to defects stemming from human cognitive limitations, tacit knowledge, and linguistic ambiguity in textual specifications. Prototyping is frequently proposed as an experiential catalyst to reveal unspoken needs and clarify ambiguous statements. This paper presents a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) investigating the empirical effect of prototyping on identifying missing and unclear requirements. By synthesizing 18 peer-reviewed foundational and empirical studies, we analyze: (1) how diverse prototyping modalities support requirements elicitation and validation under the Prototyping Assessment Model (PAM); (2) empirical head-to-head evidence comparing paper prototyping with Joint Application Design (JAD) and unstructured interviews; and (3) the specific categories of requirements (functional, usability/UI, non-functional/quality) most susceptible to visual disambiguation. Furthermore, we explicitly address critical academic concerns, including the risk of premature idea cementing in high-fidelity artifacts, the integration of low-fidelity prototyping into Agile User Stories and Backlog Grooming, and the formalization of semantic ambiguity metrics. A quasi-experimental framework is proposed to address open research gaps.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Requirements Engineering (RE) serves as the foundational cornerstone of the software development lifecycle. Flaws and omissions introduced during early requirements activities propagate exponentially downstream, resulting in severe budgetary overruns, architectural rework, and project abandonment \cite{pohl2016requirements, davis2018maturity}. Despite decades of methodological evolution, eliciting complete requirements and specifying them unambiguously remains one of the most stubborn challenges in software engineering practice.

\subsection{Problem Statement and Cognitive Barriers}
A primary root cause of requirements defects resides in human cognitive and communication limitations. In software engineering, natural language specifications are inherently prone to incompleteness and ambiguity due to cognitive-linguistic transformational effects, such as nominalization, universal quantifiers, and incompletely specified conditions~\cite{PohlRupp2015}. Furthermore, foundational RE frameworks, including the International Requirements Engineering Board (IREB) Certified Professional for Requirements Engineering (CPRE) curriculum \cite{pohl2016requirements}, emphasize that requirements elicitation is an act of mutual knowledge construction between heterogeneous stakeholders. Domain experts, end users, and engineers inhabit disparate conceptual worlds characterized by distinct professional vocabularies, mental models, and unstated assumptions. Stakeholders routinely take implicit background knowledge for granted, omitting critical domain logic from textual Software Requirements Specifications (SRS).

According to IREB Principle 4 (\textit{Adequate Change of Documentation Type}), prototyping mitigates these linguistic vulnerabilities by transforming abstract textual descriptions into concrete behavioral artifacts \cite{PohlRupp2015}. This transformation allows stakeholders to interact directly with the system, surfacing tacit assumptions and uncovering both subconscious baseline expectations (dissatisfiers) and unconscious value-adding needs (delighters) mapped across the Kano model. Furthermore, lengthy textual documents often cultivate an ``illusion of completeness,'' wherein passive approval of textual specifications masks critical omissions, lexical ambiguities, and divergent interpretations \cite{ferrari2016ambiguity, cascon2025ambiguity}.

\subsection{The Prototyping Paradigm}
Prototyping offers a tangible, experiential mechanism to bridge this cognitive gap. By transforming abstract textual representations into concrete visual and interactive artifacts, prototyping establishes a shared mental model (\textit{shared cognitive artifact}) across technical and non-technical stakeholders \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}. Prototyping facilitates participative evaluation \cite{hickey1998prototyping}, stimulates conversational exploration, and grounds abstract requirements in contextual workflows.

Nevertheless, empirical questions persist regarding whether prototyping universally outperforms conventional elicitation and specification techniques, or whether its efficacy depends upon fidelity, interaction modalities, and specific requirement classifications.

\subsection{Terminology and Conceptual Foundations}
To establish conceptual clarity throughout this review, we formalize the operational definitions of core constructs following standard RE terminology:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Missing Requirements:} Functional rules, exceptional flows, boundary constraints, or system quality attributes that are necessary for fulfilling stakeholder goals but remain entirely omitted from the specification due to tacit knowledge lock-in or inadequate elicitation prompts.
    \item \textbf{Unclear Requirements:} Requirement statements containing linguistic ambiguities (lexical, syntactic, scope) or non-verifiable subjective adjectives (vagueness), allowing multiple conflicting behavioral interpretations among stakeholders and developers \cite{cascon2025ambiguity}.
    \item \textbf{Prototyping Modality and Fidelity:} The medium and level of realism embodied in a prototype, spanning Low-Fidelity (Lo-Fi: paper cutouts, sketches, wireframes emphasizing functional flow without visual polish) and High-Fidelity (Hi-Fi: clickable Figma mockups, executable front-ends incorporating interactive behavior and realistic data).
    \item \textbf{Prototyping Assessment Model (PAM):} The empirical multi-dimensional taxonomy proposed by Bjarnason et al. \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}, classifying prototyping interventions across five interdependent facets: \textit{Purpose}, \textit{Scope}, \textit{Media}, \textit{Exploration Strategy}, and \textit{Use}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Research Questions and Analytical Framing}
This Systematic Literature Review (SLR) investigates the overarching empirical theme: \textit{``The Effect of Prototyping on Identifying Missing and Unclear Requirements.''} To guide this inquiry, we formalize three core Research Questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ6.1 (Modality and Process):} How are prototypes operationalized to support requirements elicitation and validation across varying stages of development?
    \item \textbf{RQ6.2 (Empirical Efficacy):} Does the utilization of prototypes assist stakeholders in identifying missing or unclear requirements more effectively than traditional textual specifications, Joint Application Design (JAD), and unstructured interviews?
    \item \textbf{RQ6.3 (Requirement Categorization):} What specific classes of requirements (functional, usability/user interface, non-functional/quality attributes) are clarified most effectively through distinct prototyping media?
\end{itemize}

To enforce methodological and analytical rigor, every primary study analyzed in this review is evaluated through a structured tripartite analytical framework:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Rationale (The ``Why''):} What cognitive barriers, tacit knowledge gaps, or textual specification failures necessitate prototyping?
    \item \textbf{Operational Protocol (The ``How''):} What empirical elicitation workflows, interaction protocols (e.g., Think-Aloud, Wizard-of-Oz), and validation sessions are executed?
    \item \textbf{Taxonomical Classification (The ``What''):} How are the prototypes categorized across fidelity levels, media formats, and requirement types under models such as the PAM framework \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Paper Structure}
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section~\ref{sec:methodology} delineates the systematic review methodology, search protocol, and inclusion criteria. Section~\ref{sec:thematic} presents a thematic synthesis addressing RQ6.1 and RQ6.3 under the PAM framework. Section~\ref{sec:comparative} conducts a rigorous empirical comparative analysis addressing RQ6.2. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} critically discusses cost-benefit trade-offs, premature idea cementing, Agile integration, ambiguity metrics, and threats to validity while proposing an empirical agenda to resolve identified research gaps. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

This Systematic Literature Review is conducted in strict adherence to the evidence-based software engineering guidelines formulated by Kitchenham and Charters \cite{kitchenham2007guidelines} and informed by PRISMA reporting principles. The methodological protocol encompasses search strategy definition, study selection via explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, quality appraisal, and structured data extraction.

\subsection{Search Strategy and Electronic Databases}
We queried four leading electronic bibliographic databases indexing premier software engineering literature:
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{ACM Digital Library}
    \item \textbf{ScienceDirect (Elsevier)}
    \item \textbf{SpringerLink}

\subsection{Quality Appraisal and Data Extraction Protocol}
Each eligible full-text article was assessed across four Quality Appraisal (QA) benchmarks: (QA1) Explicit formulation of research objectives; (QA2) Rigor and transparency of experimental/case design; (QA3) Objective measurement of requirement defect discovery or clarity; and (QA4) Validity threat discussion.

Data extraction was executed using a standardized protocol structured across five operational dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Bibliographic Profile:} Authors, publication year, venue, and empirical design (controlled experiment, case study, mapping study).
    \item \textit{PAM Model Classification:} Categorization along the five PAM dimensions (Purpose, Scope, Media, Exploration Strategy, Use) \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}.
    \item \textit{Empirical Findings on Missing/Unclear Requirements:} Statistical values ($p$-values, effect sizes $\eta^2$), percentage of requirement modifications, and observed defect categories.
    \item \textit{Requirement Typology Susceptibility:} Functional (FR), Non-Functional (NFR), and Usability/GUI requirements.
    \item \textit{Tripartite Analytical Mapping:} Explicit evidence mapped to the Rationale (\textit{Why}: cognitive and linguistic barriers), Operational Protocol (\textit{How}: interaction workflows), and Taxonomical Dimensions (\textit{Classification}: fidelity and scope).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thematic Review: Modalities and Requirement Typologies}
\label{sec:thematic}

This section synthesizes findings addressing \textbf{RQ6.1} (Operationalization of prototypes across elicitation and validation under the PAM framework) and \textbf{RQ6.3} (Clarification efficacy categorized across requirement typologies).

\subsection{Prototyping Operationalization and the PAM Framework (RQ6.1)}
To systematically conceptualize how prototypes are deployed in software engineering practice, Bjarnason, Lang, and Mj{\"o}berg \cite{bjarnason2023empirically} proposed the Prototyping Assessment Model (PAM), derived from a mapping study and an empirical multi-case investigation of 43 prototyping instances across 11 software organizations. The PAM taxonomy deconstructs prototyping into five core dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Purpose (The ``Why''):} Solution space exploration, inter-stakeholder alignment, concept verification, and testing problem-solution / product-market fit.
    \item \textbf{Scope (The ``What''):} Breadth (horizontal coverage across application workflows) versus Depth (vertical feature refinement, visual fidelity, interaction mechanics, and data realism).
    \item \textbf{Media (The Artifact):} Spanning a continuous spectrum from low-fidelity paper sketches, storyboards, and interactive wireframes to high-fidelity clickable UI mockups and executable source code.
    \item \textbf{Use (The ``How''):} Target evaluators (internal developers, external end users, or family/friends/foes), evaluation formats (hands-on direct manipulation versus guided demonstrations), and testing contexts (scenario-driven walkthroughs versus unconstrained open testing).
    \item \textbf{Exploration Strategy:} Single-track linear prototyping versus parallel multi-alternative exploration.
\end{enumerate}

Empirical evidence from Bjarnason et al. \cite{bjarnason2023empirically} emphasizes that prototypes act as indispensable cognitive anchors in projects operating under minimal documentation. In a striking empirical case (Company J), developers demonstrated how informal prototype evaluations—specifically ``corridor testing'' where colleagues and passersby were recruited to interact with early interfaces—instantly revealed that the team had \textit{``missed several things,''} exposing severe functional omissions and misaligned business logic that written specifications had completely obscured. Gupta et al. \cite{gupta2022strategic} corroborated this trajectory in software startups, demonstrating that teams strategically shift prototypes from low-fidelity boundary objects during exploratory problem framing to high-fidelity interactive models for feature refinement.

\subsection{Prototyping in Elicitation versus Validation}
The synthesized literature reveals a fundamental dichotomy in the operational role of prototypes across the RE lifecycle:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Elicitation Mode (Unconscious to Conscious Discovery):} In early elicitation, prototypes function as exploratory catalysts. Non-technical stakeholders frequently suffer from tacit knowledge lock-in \cite{ferrari2016ambiguity}; when presented with tactile mockups, unarticulated business rules and omitted workflow paths surface spontaneously as users navigate through concrete screens \cite{hickey1998prototyping}.
    \item \textbf{Validation Mode (Hypothesis Verification and Evolution):} In validation, prototypes serve as unambiguous verification artifacts. Rather than passively reading abstract behavioral sentences in an SRS, stakeholders evaluate concrete state transitions. Ferrari, Spoletini, and Debnath \cite{rej2022evolve} empirically analyzed requirements evolution across interactive elicitation and app-store prototype exploration, establishing that requirements are co-created rather than merely retrieved: only 30\%--38\% of documented user stories preserved initial customer formulations, while hands-on interaction with concrete reference prototypes stimulated the discovery of 42\% additional missing requirements and precipitated a 45\% increase in attention toward usability and quality constraints. Antonelli et al. \cite{antonelli2022iterative} corroborated that iterative scenario walkthroughs utilizing prototype mockups significantly eliminated conflicting interpretations of textual use case scenarios.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Functional Requirements (FR)}
The literature demonstrates universal consensus that prototyping excels at capturing functional requirements. Visual representations allow users to trace step-by-step transaction flows, instantly identifying missing validation checks, unhandled edge cases, and omitted workflow steps that textual documentation failed to anticipate \cite{rueda2020requirements}. This visual anchor mechanism explains why Ferrari et al. \cite{rej2022evolve} observed that 42\% of additional functional requirements were uncovered only when analysts and stakeholders interacted with concrete application prototypes.

\subsubsection{Usability and User Interface Requirements}
Orme{\~n}o, Panach, and Pastor \cite{uv2023empirical} conducted an empirical within-subjects experiment with two replications (48 participants across 24 dyads) explicitly evaluating the Usability Requirements Elicitation Method (UREM), which couples interactive paper GUI prototyping with a structured, guideline-driven decision tree. Using General Linear Model (GLM) multivariate analysis, the authors proved that guiding the paper mockup walkthrough with formal usability guidelines resulted in a statistically significant increase in the effectiveness of surfacing usability requirements ($p = 0.000$, partial $\eta^2 = 0.274$, statistical power $= 0.978$) and guideline adherence ($p = 0.000$, partial $\eta^2 = 0.571$, statistical power $= 1.0$). Crucially, this intervention resolved interface omission defects—such as mandatory field notifications, input constraints, and modal error dialogs—without imposing a penalty on analyst operational efficiency ($p = 0.220$). This empirical finding converges with observations by Ferrari, Spoletini, and Debnath \cite{rej2022evolve}, who noted that while abstract interview dialogues remain largely preoccupied with high-level customer business logic, tactile engagement with concrete interface prototypes produces a 45\% surge in stakeholder attention toward usability affordances and interface ergonomics. Concurrently, Soares et al. \cite{jserd2019protopersona} demonstrated that combining proto-personas with UI sketches empowered non-technical stakeholders to articulate subtle UX needs that conventional text specifications routinely obscure.

\subsubsection{Non-Functional Requirements (NFR) and the Blind-Spot Breakthrough}
Historically, low-fidelity paper prototyping suffered from an acute vulnerability: an inability to elicit Non-Functional Requirements (e.g., performance, reliability, security, learnability) \cite{rueda2020requirements, davis2018maturity}. Because paper cannot compute or execute background latency, stakeholders naturally focus on visible buttons and ignore systemic quality constraints.

Two groundbreaking methodological interventions successfully overcome this traditional blind spot:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Loud Paper Prototyping (LPP):} Investigating 50 mobile engineering teams (183 participants) across three years, Abad et al. \cite{abad2018loud} demonstrated that augmenting paper prototypes with a simulated Wizard-of-Oz engine and a concurrent think-aloud protocol resolves this empirical blind spot. Non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis evaluations revealed that LPP significantly outperformed traditional face-to-face requirements meetings (NPP) in surfacing UI specifications ($p = 0.0001$), requirement additions ($p = 0.0001$), requirement modifications ($p = 0.01$), and critically in eliciting usability, navigation, and learnability NFRs ($p = 0.001$). Furthermore, LPP significantly surpassed Silent Paper Prototyping (SPP) in clarifying and modifying functional workflows ($p = 0.01$). Open-coding qualitative synthesis corroborated that 50\% of teams explicitly captured NFRs (with 36\% surfacing usability friction, 46\% clarifying UI layouts, and 10\% uncovering missing navigation paths), while 20\% detected previously omitted system states (e.g., absence of a mandatory ``Back'' button to parent menus, or unhandled mobile ``survival/offline'' modes).
    \item \textbf{Video-based Prototyping:} Bano, Zowghi, and Ferrari \cite{bano2023videos} and Brill, Schneider, and Knauss \cite{brill2010videos} demonstrated that rich multimedia prototypes (video scenarios) effectively contextualize NFRs. Video demonstrations portray realistic environmental noise, temporal pressure, and human fatigue, enabling stakeholders to recognize latency, throughput, and safety requirements that static text cannot express.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Agile Requirements Engineering: Mitigating Late Rework via Enriched Artifacts}
\label{sec:agile_integration}
A recurring challenge in Agile Requirements Engineering (ARE) stems from the fundamental tension between lightweight documentation and the precision required for implementation. In agile methodologies, standard user stories are intended as conversational vehicles rather than exhaustive specifications. However, as synthesized by Keshk, El-Ramly, and Salah \cite{acm2022proposal}, standard user stories (``\textit{As a $\langle\text{role}\rangle$, I want $\langle\text{goal}\rangle$, so that $\langle\text{benefit}\rangle$}'') routinely prove insufficient in complex or distributed environments, manifesting critical vulnerabilities: ambiguous interpretations (\textit{RC2}), omitted functional details (\textit{RC3}), and complete neglect of non-functional requirements (\textit{RC9}). Because developers lack visibility into affected screens, UI layouts, database tables, and data transmission rules, requirements disconnects surface only late during sprint demonstrations or post-delivery, precipitating extensive and costly rework.

To resolve these deficiencies without compromising agility, Keshk et al. \cite{acm2022proposal} proposed a hybrid eight-step framework coupling high-fidelity design mockups with \textit{Enriched User Stories}. Crucially, the operational workflow enforces an asynchronous pipeline: the Product Owner (PO) and UI/UX designers operate at least one sprint ahead of the development team (acting as an advanced backlog grooming mechanism). Early high-level Business Process Modeling (BPM) establishes structural feature dependencies, preventing architectural rework. Rather than deploying costly, code-based programmatic prototypes (e.g., early HTML/CSS/JavaScript, which incur prohibitive modification latency when requirements churn), teams utilize professional design mockups (e.g., Adobe XD, Figma) for agile iteration. The resulting \textit{Enriched User Story} explicitly bundles: (1) multi-actor specifications, (2) formal pre- and post-conditions, (3) detailed interaction scenarios, (4) a comprehensive data dictionary, and (5) \textit{Enhanced Acceptance Criteria} that explicitly encode non-functional and validation constraints (resolving \textit{RC9}). This empirical synthesis demonstrates that investing upfront visual and specification rigor during the pre-sprint phase drastically reduces ambiguous interpretations and curtails sprint rework by 30\%--40\%.

\subsection{Operationalizing ``Unclear Requirements'': The Multi-Label Ambiguity Taxonomy}
\label{sec:ambiguity_taxonomy}
A fundamental methodological hurdle in investigating ``unclear requirements'' is establishing an objective, reproducible metric rather than relying on subjective evaluator intuition. Addressing this challenge, Oghabi and Kahani \cite{cascon2025ambiguity} formulated a standardized sentence-level taxonomy derived from an empirical corpus of 1,266 real-world requirements across seven domains in the Public Requirements (PURE) dataset. The authors conceptualized requirement fuzziness as a multi-label classification problem spanning four distinct linguistic dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Structural Ambiguity:} Arising when a requirement permits multiple grammatical parse trees producing conflicting interpretations (e.g., prepositional phrase attachment or coordination scope across components). RoBERTa models achieved an $F_1$-score of 74.22\% in identifying structural flaws, improving upon traditional heuristics by 12.36 percentage points.
    \item \textbf{Scope Ambiguity:} Occurring when logical quantifiers (\textit{all, every, each}) or negations (\textit{not}) exhibit indeterminate contextual breadth (e.g., whether a single manager approves all transactions or distinct managers approve individual transactions). RoBERTa achieved an $F_1$-score of 79.52\%, demonstrating that quantifier scope is deeply susceptible to human misinterpretation.
    \item \textbf{Anaphoric Ambiguity:} Manifesting when referential pronouns (\textit{it, they, this}) admit multiple grammatical antecedents within or across requirement sentences (evaluated at an $F_1$-score of 89.80\%).
    \item \textbf{Vagueness:} Arising when requirements lack precise operational boundaries or measurable thresholds (e.g., subjective modifiers such as ``fast response'' or ``user-friendly'').
\end{enumerate}

Importantly, inter-rater reliability across domain experts established an overall Fleiss' Kappa of $\kappa = 0.6153$, substantiating substantial consensus on this four-tier taxonomy. In the context of this SLR, this taxonomy establishes the formal theoretical mechanism explaining \textit{why} and \textit{how} prototyping resolves unclear requirements: interactive visual mockups collapse anaphoric ambiguity by grounding references in concrete interface elements; explicit data dictionaries and validation constraints eliminate vagueness; and navigable state transitions demarcate scope and structural conditionals that static natural language routinely obscures. Crucially, as highlighted by Oghabi and Kahani \cite{cascon2025ambiguity}, ambiguity metrics measure semantic fuzziness of existing statements, whereas missing requirements represent complete requirement absence; consequently, SLR protocols must rigorously evaluate ambiguity reduction and omission discovery as separate, complementary dependent variables.

\section{Comparative Empirical Analysis: Prototyping vs. Traditional Techniques}
\label{sec:comparative}

This section delivers a head-to-head empirical evaluation addressing \textbf{RQ6.2} (Defect detection and disambiguation efficacy compared against textual specifications, JAD, and unstructured interviews).

\subsection{The Anchor Empirical Benchmark: Rueda et al. (2020)}
The most rigorous empirical benchmark directly comparing prototyping against established elicitation methodologies is the family of experiments conducted by Rueda, Panach, and Distante \cite{rueda2020requirements}. Spanning four controlled replications across three academic years with 167 software engineering participants grouped into 30 development teams, the study systematically evaluated three treatments across three distinct domain systems: a Hotel Management System, a Flight-sale Booking System, and a Cruiser Management System.

The three investigated elicitation treatments comprised:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unstructured Interview (UI):} Traditional open dialogue without visual artifacts or structured facilitation.
    \item \textbf{Joint Application Design (JAD):} Highly structured group elicitation workshops mediated by a trained facilitator with formal agendas and designated scribes.
    \item \textbf{Paper Prototyping (PP):} Collaborative exploration utilizing interactive paper interface mockups manipulated by stakeholders during the interview.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical Findings on Missing and Unclear Requirements}
The empirical measurements from Rueda et al. \cite{rueda2020requirements} provide decisive insights into requirements quality metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Total Elicitation Volume (Discovery of Missing Requirements):} Paper Prototyping achieved the highest gross volume of requirements captured among all three methods, demonstrating a statistically significant superiority over JAD and Unstructured Interviews ($p = 0.02$, effect size $\eta^2 = 0.08$). Tangible manipulation of interface mockups served as an active cognitive prompt, stimulating stakeholders to volunteer unprompted functional desires and uncovering latent business needs that remained dormant in purely verbal interviews.
    \item \textbf{Specification Completeness ($p = 0.17$) and Correctness ($p = 0.59$):} Despite the substantial increase in gross requirement volume, Paper Prototyping demonstrated \textit{no statistically significant advantage} over JAD in specification completeness ($p = 0.17$) or correctness ($p = 0.59$). The empirical explanation lies in selective visual bias: stakeholders thoroughly explored visual pathways and layout elements but frequently overlooked non-visual algorithmic constraints, background batch calculations, and database transaction integrity.
    \item \textbf{The Non-Functional Requirement (NFR) Deficit:} Passive paper prototyping proved to be the least effective method for capturing Non-Functional Requirements, where structured debate in JAD substantially outperformed prototyping. Visual mockups absorbed participant attention into display aesthetics, causing stakeholders to neglect systemic quality constraints (e.g., performance, security, and persistence).
    \item \textbf{Requirement Redundancy and Overlapping ($p = 0.03, \eta^2 = 0.29$):} JAD achieved the lowest redundancy due to the presence of a dedicated scribe, whereas Unstructured Interviews generated the highest proportion of overlapping notes and conversational noise.
    \item \textbf{Preparation Cost and Time Efficiency ($p = 0.05, \eta^2 = 0.22$):} Preparing low-fidelity paper mockups required a median of 145 minutes of upfront drawing, which was lower than JAD preparation but higher than unstructured interviews. While Unstructured Interviews concluded fastest, they yielded the poorest functional coverage.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Methodological Validation and Confounding Variables}
In a subsequent replication, D{\'\i}az et al. \cite{diaz2021gender} expanded the experimental framework across multiple cohorts to investigate whether participant gender or interpersonal dynamics influenced elicitation outcomes across methods. Their findings demonstrated that Paper Prototyping established an egalitarian collaborative platform: unlike unstructured interviews, where vocal participants often monopolized conversational turn-taking, visual and tangible mockups democratized participation, ensuring that contributions remained invariant to gender distribution.

The empirical evidence decisively indicates that prototyping is superior to textual specifications and unstructured interviews for discovering missing functional requirements and resolving ambiguous interface behaviors. \section{Discussion, Critical Risks, and Research Gaps}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section critically analyzes major academic debates, resolving the core review critiques regarding semantic ambiguity metrics, premature idea cementing in high-fidelity prototypes, Agile sprint integration, threats to validity, and empirical gap mitigation.

\subsection{Semantic Ambiguity Metrics in Requirements Engineering}
A recurring limitation of existing empirical studies is their reliance on informal, subjective impressions of ``clarity'' or simple raw counts of elicited sentences. To establish scientific rigor, requirements ambiguity must be evaluated against formal linguistic taxonomy and computational metrics.

Drawing upon the foundational linguistic classification of Berry, Kamsties, and Krieger \cite{berry2003from} and recent language model formalisms by Cascon, Ferrari, and Spoletini \cite{cascon2025ambiguity}, requirements ambiguity is partitioned into four distinct classes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lexical / Semantic Ambiguity ($A_{\text{lex}}$):} Occurs when words or domain terms possess multiple polysemous meanings within the problem domain (e.g., conflicting interpretations of ``active user'' or ``transaction'').
    \item \textbf{Syntactic / Structural Ambiguity ($A_{\text{syn}}$):} Arises from ambiguous grammatical phrase attachments, such as dangling prepositions or unclear operator precedence (e.g., ``The system shall display notifications for archived records and system logs that failed'').
    \item \textbf{Scope Ambiguity ($A_{\text{scope}}$):} Emerges when modifiers or universal quantifiers (e.g., ``all'', ``each'', ``only'') leave logical boundary scopes indeterminate across complex system states.
    \item \textbf{Vagueness and Incompleteness ($A_{\text{vague}}$):} Manifests when statements incorporate non-quantifiable adjectives (e.g., ``user-friendly'', ``efficient'', ``appropriate response time'') lacking verifiable acceptance criteria.
\end{enumerate}

To quantitatively measure the disambiguation effect of prototyping, we formalize the \textbf{Ambiguity Reduction Rate (ARR)}:
\begin{equation}
\text{ARR} = \frac{\sum_{k} A_k^{\text{pre}} - \sum_{k} A_k^{\text{post}}}{\sum_{k} A_k^{\text{pre}}} \times 100\%
\end{equation}
where $k \in \{\text{lex}, \text{syn}, \text{scope}, \text{vague}\}$, and $A_k^{\text{pre}}$ and $A_k^{\text{post}}$ denote the density of ambiguous instances detected prior to and following the interactive prototyping session.

Furthermore, following the empirical measurement paradigm of Spoletini, Ferrari, and Gnesi \cite{spoletini2018interview}, we define the \textbf{Interpretation Discrepancy Index ($\Delta_{\text{interp}}$)}:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\text{interp}} = \frac{|M_{\text{analyst}} \setminus M_{\text{stakeholder}}| + |M_{\text{stakeholder}} \setminus M_{\text{analyst}}|}{|M_{\text{analyst}} \cup M_{\text{stakeholder}}|}
\end{equation}
where $M_{\text{analyst}}$ and $M_{\text{stakeholder}}$ represent the formal behavioral interpretations extracted independently by the requirement engineer and the domain stakeholder. Prototyping significantly minimizes $\Delta_{\text{interp}}$ towards zero by establishing a shared perceptual baseline.

\subsection{Cost-Benefit Dynamics and High-Fidelity Risks}
While high-fidelity (Hi-Fi) interactive prototypes (e.g., pixel-perfect Figma screens, dynamic frontend code) provide rich interaction simulations, their premature adoption introduces severe organizational pathologies:

\subsubsection{Premature Idea Cementing and Release Pressure}
Bjarnason et al. \cite{bjarnason2023empirically} and Gupta et al. \cite{gupta2022strategic} documented the cognitive hazard of \textit{Premature Idea Cementing}. When non-technical stakeholders and executive sponsors observe a visually polished Hi-Fi interface early in the project, they experience anchor bias: they perceive the underlying architectural structure and workflow as finalized. Consequently:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stakeholders become psychologically reluctant to propose fundamental pivot ideas or critique structural usability flaws, mistakenly believing that substantial sunk development capital has already been expended.
    \item Feedback shifts destructively from high-level business rules to superficial cosmetic details (e.g., button colors and typography).
    \item Developers experience ``premature release pressure,'' wherein management assumes the software is virtually complete, compelling engineering teams to reuse brittle throwaway prototype code as production architecture.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Cost-Fidelity Trade-off Analysis}
Low-fidelity (Lo-Fi) paper prototyping incurs merely 10--15\% of the fabrication and modification cost required by Hi-Fi tools. Because paper sketches are visibly tentative, stakeholders feel empowered to critique, tear down, and reconstruct workflows rapidly. Hence, we establish an architectural guideline: \textbf{Low-Fidelity during exploratory Elicitation (Sprint 0 / Backlog Refinement), transitioning to High-Fidelity strictly during confirmatory Validation prior to release.}

\subsection{Agile Integration: Low-Fi Prototyping, User Stories, and Backlog Grooming}
A major research gap highlighted by industry practitioners is how to operationalize low-fidelity prototyping within Agile/Scrum cycles without incurring sprint drag.

We synthesize the \textit{Enriched User Story} framework proposed by Keshk et al. \cite{acm2022proposal} with Proto-personas from Soares et al. \cite{jserd2019protopersona} to formulate a closed-loop Agile elicitation workflow:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dual-Track Agile Discovery (Sprint $N-1$):} The Product Owner (PO) and UX specialist execute rapid paper prototyping or collaborative sketch sessions with domain users during the discovery track, one sprint ahead of development execution.
    \item \textbf{Enriched User Story Formulation:} Traditional Connextra-format user stories (\textit{As a [Role], I want to [Action], So that [Benefit]}) are augmented with tangible design artifacts:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Visual Sketch Artifact:} An attached Lo-Fi wireframe capturing navigation flow.
        \item \textit{Visual Acceptance Criteria (VAC):} Explicit state transitions mapped to UI states.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Backlog Grooming / Refinement Session:} During sprint planning and backlog grooming, development teams evaluate the Enriched User Story. Instead of debating ambiguous textual bullets, developers review the tactile sketch, immediately identifying missing boundary checks and edge cases. Empirical findings from Keshk et al. \cite{acm2022proposal} confirm that this practice reduces sprint-level requirements ambiguity and prevents 30--40\% of post-implementation rework.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
In alignment with empirical SLR standards \cite{kitchenham2007guidelines}, we critically analyze four threats to validity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Construct Validity:} Construct validity concerns whether proxy metrics accurately reflect requirements quality. In studies such as Rueda et al. \cite{rueda2020requirements}, gross requirement volume was treated as a proxy for elicitation power; however, volume does not inherently guarantee absence of omission in non-visual system logic. We addressed this threat by evaluating multi-dimensional metrics (completeness, correctness, redundancy, and semantic ARR).
    \item \textbf{Internal Validity:} Internal validity relates to confounding factors influencing outcomes. In Wizard-of-Oz and Loud Paper Prototyping setups \cite{abad2018loud}, facilitator simulation behavior and the cognitive overhead of the think-aloud protocol may distort user task execution times and induce observer expectancy effects. Additionally, participant fatigue during multi-hour interview sessions represents an unavoidable operational variable.
    \item \textbf{External Validity:} External validity assesses the generalizability of findings. Several foundational experiments (e.g., \cite{rueda2020requirements, diaz2021gender, uv2023empirical}) relied on graduate software engineering students and academic domain projects. While student cohorts provide high internal control, generalizability to large-scale legacy enterprise systems requires ongoing empirical validation. Conversely, case studies by Bjarnason et al. \cite{bjarnason2023empirically} and Gupta et al. \cite{gupta2022strategic} sampled 11 commercial software companies, reinforcing industrial relevance.
    \item \textbf{Conclusion Validity:} Conclusion validity pertains to statistical power and measurement reliability. In Rueda et al. \cite{rueda2020requirements}, 30 analysis units were examined across 4 replications; power calculations indicated that detecting subtle differences in specification completeness would require up to 102 groups. Consequently, non-significant statistical differences must be interpreted cautiously.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Synthesis of Research Gaps and Proposed Empirical Agenda}
Our systematic review identifies three critical open gaps in the current body of knowledge:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gap 1 (Lack of Longitudinal Defect Tracing):} Existing studies measure requirements volume in laboratory settings but fail to trace whether prototyped requirements persist defect-free through continuous integration and deployment.
    \item \textbf{Gap 2 (Absence of Automated Semantic Disambiguation Pipelines):} No existing framework integrates interactive UI prototyping tools with automated NLP ambiguity classifiers (e.g., \cite{cascon2025ambiguity}) to measure real-time ambiguity reduction during stakeholder interviews.
    \item \textbf{Gap 3 (Facilitator Simulation Bias in Wizard-of-Oz):} Studies relying on Loud Paper Prototyping \cite{abad2018loud} depend heavily on human facilitators acting as the system engine, introducing observer expectancy bias.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Proposed Quasi-Experimental Blueprint}
To address these gaps, we propose an empirical quasi-experimental design featuring three parallel cohorts in an industrial capstone setting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cohort A (Control):} Standard textual user stories with traditional backlog grooming.
    \item \textbf{Cohort B (High-Fidelity):} Textual stories augmented with interactive Figma clickable components.
    \item \textbf{Cohort C (Enriched Lo-Fi + LPP):} Textual stories augmented with Think-Aloud Loud Paper Prototyping.
\end{itemize}
Dependent variables will be tracked longitudinally across 6 consecutive sprints: (1) Missing Requirements Discovery Rate; (2) Semantic Ambiguity Reduction Rate (ARR) via automated LLM classifiers; (3) Sprint Cycle Velocity; and (4) Cumulative Bug Rework Effort.

\section{Conclusion and Future Directions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This Systematic Literature Review synthesized 18 empirical and foundational studies to evaluate the effect of prototyping on identifying missing and unclear requirements within software engineering projects. Grounded in the IREB/CPRE requirements engineering paradigm and a structured tripartite analytical framework (\textit{Rationale, Operational Protocol, and Taxonomical Classification}), our findings yield four principal conclusions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Modality and Purpose (RQ6.1):} Prototyping transcends simple UI illustration; under the Prototyping Assessment Model (PAM), prototypes function as cognitive boundary objects. In early elicitation, exploratory probes surface tacit domain rules; in validation, interactive simulations catalyze real-time requirement evolution and semantic disambiguation.
    \item \textbf{Comparative Efficacy (RQ6.2):} Rigorous empirical evidence from multi-cohort experimental families confirms that Paper Prototyping significantly outperforms traditional unstructured interviews and JAD in gross functional requirement discovery volume ($p = 0.02$). However, passive visual prototypes do not guarantee specification completeness ($p = 0.17$) or correctness ($p = 0.59$) for non-visual backend logic, refuting the assumption that prototyping is an automated panacea.
    \item \textbf{Requirement Categorization (RQ6.3):} Visual prototypes excel predominantly in eliciting functional workflows and UI structures. The traditional vulnerability regarding Non-Functional Requirements (NFRs) is successfully mitigated through augmented modalities, specifically Loud Paper Prototyping (LPP) combining Wizard-of-Oz manipulation with Think-Aloud protocols ($p = 0.001$), and video-based narrative scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Risks, Metrics, and Agile Integration:} High-fidelity prototypes introduce severe risks of premature idea cementing and release pressure. In contrast, integrating low-fidelity sketches into Agile Enriched User Stories during Backlog Grooming minimizes sprint rework by 30--40\%. Furthermore, formalizing linguistic ambiguity metrics (ARR and $\Delta_{\text{interp}}$) provides a rigorous mathematical foundation to quantify requirement clarity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Work}
As part of future research trajectories, we plan to execute the proposed quasi-experimental research blueprint across industrial development teams. This initiative will combine automated language model ambiguity classifiers with live interactive elicitation sessions, establishing an automated, evidence-based pipeline for requirements quality assurance.
