\section{Research gaps}
\label{sec:gaps}

This section examines open research gaps identified in the literature, focusing on ambiguity measurement, risks associated with high-fidelity prototypes, agile integration, and threats to validity.

\subsection{Categorizing and resolving semantic ambiguity}
A recurring limitation of earlier empirical studies is their reliance on informal, subjective impressions of ``clarity'' or simple raw counts of elicited sentences. To establish scientific rigor, requirements ambiguity must be evaluated against formal linguistic classifications.

Drawing upon seminal linguistic foundations in Requirements Engineering established by Berry, Kamsties, and Krieger \cite{berry2003contract} and formalized by Cascon et al. \cite{cascon2025ambiguity}, requirements ambiguity is categorized into four distinct classes: (1) \textit{lexical and semantic ambiguity}, where words or domain terms possess multiple meanings within the problem domain (e.g., conflicting interpretations of ``active customer'' or ``storage reservation''); (2) \textit{syntactic and structural ambiguity}, involving ambiguous grammatical structures such as dangling prepositions or unclear logical combinations of conditionals; (3) \textit{scope ambiguity}, arising when quantifiers (e.g., ``all'', ``each'', ``only'') leave operational boundaries indeterminate across system states; and (4) \textit{vagueness and incompleteness}, characterized by subjective, non-quantifiable adjectives (e.g., ``user-friendly'', ``appropriate response time'') that lack verifiable acceptance criteria.

Berry et al. \cite{berry2003contract} noted that unconscious disambiguation is especially risky: readers adopt an intuitive meaning and assume that consensus exists without considering alternative interpretations. Visual mockups help mitigate these ambiguities by tying pronouns and terms to concrete interface elements, while screen layouts clarify input boundaries and validation rules \cite{ferrari2016ambiguity, cascon2025ambiguity, berry2003contract}. As Spoletini et al. \cite{spoletini2018interview} observed, prototypes act as shared reference points that reconcile differing interpretations between analysts and stakeholders.

\subsection{Cost-benefit dynamics and high-fidelity risks}
While high-fidelity (Hi-Fi) interactive prototypes (e.g., clickable Figma screens, interactive front-ends) provide realistic user experience simulations, their premature adoption introduces significant organizational and cognitive risks.

A principal cognitive barrier is \textit{premature idea cementing}. When stakeholders and sponsors observe a polished, pixel-perfect interface early in the project, they anchor prematurely on the visual presentation \cite{bjarnason2023empirically, gupta2022strategic}. Consequently, stakeholders often become reluctant to propose structural alterations or challenge core business logic, mistakenly assuming the underlying architecture is finalized.

This vulnerability is directly explained by the Economic Principle of Prototyping formulated by Lim, Stolterman, and Tenenberg \cite{lim2008anatomy}: the most effective prototype is the simplest manifestation that filters the necessary design dimensions. High-fidelity prototypes violate this principle early in the project by manifesting superficial aesthetic dimensions before functional core behaviors are stabilized. In parallel, high-fidelity prototypes induce \textit{premature release pressure}, where management perceives surface visual completion as engineering readiness, exerting pressure on development teams to deliver prematurely or to reuse fragile prototype mockups in production environments.

From an operational cost perspective, low-fidelity paper prototypes require only a modest upfront investment (median preparation of 145 minutes \cite{rueda2020requirements}, slashes to seconds via GenAI-assisted synthesis \cite{wei2023boosting}) and encourage stakeholders to critique and discard tentative concepts easily. Therefore, a prudent software engineering guideline is: \textit{deploy low-fidelity prototypes during exploratory elicitation and problem framing, transitioning to high-fidelity prototypes only during confirmatory validation before development}.

\subsection{Agile integration: mitigating late-stage rework}
A practical challenge in agile software development is that standard user stories (\textit{As a [Role], I want [Action], So that [Benefit]}) are often too concise, leaving critical UI layouts, data fields, and non-functional constraints unarticulated. Inayat et al. \cite{inayat2015systematic} synthesized empirical evidence demonstrating that this minimal documentation frequently triggers severe customer communication gaps, unhandled quality attributes, and extensive sprint rework during backlog demonstrations.

To bridge this gap without compromising agility, recent empirical evidence supports coupling prototypes with user stories into \textit{Enriched User Stories} \cite{acm2022proposal}. In this workflow, the Product Owner and UI/UX designer work one sprint ahead of developers during backlog grooming. By attaching low-fidelity wireframes and visual acceptance criteria directly to user stories, teams clarify data fields and edge cases before coding begins, effectively reducing downstream sprint rework by 30\%--40\% \cite{acm2022proposal}.

Furthermore, addressing how agile teams acquire and refine prototyping competencies, Lending et al. \cite{lending2022rubric} developed a standardized 5-level rubric across 8 core elicitation dimensions. In an empirical study of 45 student-client elicitation projects, Lending et al. showed that rubric-guided training dramatically increased visualization competence from a baseline mean of 1.06 to 3.31 on a 5-point scale ($p < 0.001$). In particular, the rubric guides students from Level 2 (using prototypes passively for presentation) toward Level 4 (using prototypes interactively to uncover tacit requirements and handle edge cases).

\subsection{Threats to validity}
We examine four potential threats to validity:

Regarding \textit{construct validity}, several studies treat gross requirement volume as a proxy for elicitation success. However, volume alone does not ensure completeness of non-visual backend logic; we mitigated this by synthesizing multi-dimensional metrics (completeness, correctness, typology). In terms of \textit{internal validity}, facilitator behavior and think-aloud overhead in interactive settings \cite{abad2018loud} may influence participant pacing and task execution.

For \textit{external validity}, primary controlled experiments \cite{rueda2020requirements, diaz2021gender, uv2023empirical} were conducted predominantly with student cohorts and academic domains; while offering high experimental control, findings should be corroborated across enterprise environments. Finally, regarding \textit{conclusion validity}, controlled experiments with modest sample sizes (e.g., 30 teams in Rueda et al. \cite{rueda2020requirements}) exhibit limited statistical power for detecting subtle completeness differences, requiring cautious interpretation of non-significant $p$-values.

\subsection{Identified research gaps}
Three main research gaps emerge from the reviewed literature:
\textit{Gap 1 (Ambiguity measurement)}: Most comparative studies report total numbers of requirements captured rather than measuring ambiguity reduction across formal linguistic categories \cite{berry2003contract, cascon2025ambiguity}.
\textit{Gap 2 (Longitudinal agile outcomes)}: While agile literature suggests that visual artifacts reduce rework, empirical tracking of defect propagation across multiple sprints with Enriched User Stories \cite{acm2022proposal, inayat2015systematic} remains limited.
\textit{Gap 3 (Academic and industry divide)}: Most controlled experiments rely on student cohorts, whereas industry studies primarily use qualitative interviews. Objective models such as Lending et al.'s rubric \cite{lending2022rubric} can help bridge this gap by benchmarking elicitation skills systematically.
