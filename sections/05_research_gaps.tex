\section{Research Gaps}
\label{sec:gaps}

This section critically analyzes major research gaps emerging from the synthesized literature, resolving key concerns regarding semantic ambiguity metrics, premature idea cementing in high-fidelity prototypes, Agile sprint integration, and threats to validity.

\subsection{Categorizing and Resolving Semantic Ambiguity}
A recurring limitation of earlier empirical studies is their reliance on informal, subjective impressions of ``clarity'' or simple raw counts of elicited sentences. To establish scientific rigor, requirements ambiguity must be evaluated against formal linguistic classifications.

Drawing upon linguistic foundations in Requirements Engineering \cite{berry2003from, cascon2025ambiguity}, requirements ambiguity is categorized into four distinct classes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lexical / Semantic Ambiguity:} Words or domain terms possessing multiple meanings within the problem domain (e.g., conflicting interpretations of ``active user'' or ``transaction status'').
    \item \textbf{Syntactic / Structural Ambiguity:} Ambiguous grammatical structures, such as dangling prepositions or unclear logical combinations of ``AND/OR'' conditionals.
    \item \textbf{Scope Ambiguity:} Ambiguity arising when quantifiers (e.g., ``all'', ``each'', ``only'') leave operational boundaries indeterminate across system states.
    \item \textbf{Vagueness and Incompleteness:} Statements incorporating non-quantifiable subjective adjectives (e.g., ``user-friendly'', ``efficient'', ``appropriate response time'') that lack verifiable acceptance criteria.
\end{enumerate}

Prototyping serves as an experiential filter across these linguistic categories: interactive visual mockups collapse pronoun and scope ambiguities by grounding interactions in concrete UI elements, while explicit screen layouts eliminate vagueness by forcing concrete inputs and measurable validation rules \cite{ferrari2016ambiguity, cascon2025ambiguity}. Furthermore, as emphasized by Spoletini et al. \cite{spoletini2018interview}, prototypes provide an objective reference anchor that reconciles divergent interpretations between analysts and stakeholders.

\subsection{Cost-Benefit Dynamics and High-Fidelity Risks}
While high-fidelity (Hi-Fi) interactive prototypes (e.g., clickable Figma screens, interactive front-ends) provide realistic user experience simulations, their premature adoption introduces significant organizational and cognitive risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Premature Idea Cementing:} When stakeholders and sponsors observe a polished, pixel-perfect interface early in the project, they anchor prematurely on the design \cite{bjarnason2023empirically, gupta2022strategic}. Stakeholders often become reluctant to propose structural changes or challenge core business logic, mistakenly assuming the system architecture is finalized.
    \item \textbf{Premature Release Pressure:} Management may perceive visual completion as engineering readiness, exerting pressure on development teams to deliver prematurely or to reuse fragile prototype code in production.
    \item \textbf{Cost-Fidelity Trade-offs:} Low-fidelity (Lo-Fi) paper prototypes require only a fraction of the preparation time (median of 145 minutes \cite{rueda2020requirements}) and encourage stakeholders to critique and discard tentative concepts easily. Therefore, a prudent software engineering guideline is: \textit{deploy Low-Fidelity prototypes during exploratory elicitation and problem framing, transitioning to High-Fidelity prototypes only during confirmatory validation before development}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Agile Integration: Mitigating Late-Stage Rework}
A practical challenge in agile software development is that standard user stories (\textit{As a [Role], I want [Action], So that [Benefit]}) are often too concise, leaving critical UI layouts, data fields, and non-functional constraints unarticulated. This disconnect leads to substantial rework during sprint demonstrations.

To bridge this gap without compromising agility, recent empirical evidence supports coupling prototypes with user stories into \textit{Enriched User Stories} \cite{acm2022proposal}. In this workflow, the Product Owner and UI/UX designer work one sprint ahead of developers during backlog grooming. By attaching low-fidelity wireframes and visual acceptance criteria directly to user stories, teams clarify data fields and edge cases before coding begins, effectively reducing downstream sprint rework by 30\%--40\% \cite{acm2022proposal}.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
Following evidence-based guidelines \cite{kitchenham2007guidelines}, we evaluate four validity threats:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Construct Validity:} Several studies treat gross requirement volume as a proxy for elicitation success. However, volume alone does not ensure completeness of non-visual backend logic. We mitigated this by synthesizing multi-dimensional metrics (completeness, correctness, typology).
    \item \textbf{Internal Validity:} In interactive settings (e.g., Wizard-of-Oz or Think-Aloud \cite{abad2018loud}), facilitator behavior and verbalization overhead may influence participant pacing and task execution.
    \item \textbf{External Validity:} Primary controlled experiments (e.g., \cite{rueda2020requirements, diaz2021gender, uv2023empirical}) were conducted with student cohorts and academic domains. While offering high experimental control, findings should be corroborated in large-scale industry environments.
    \item \textbf{Conclusion Validity:} Controlled experiments with modest cohorts (e.g., 30 teams in Rueda et al. \cite{rueda2020requirements}) exhibit limited power for detecting subtle completeness differences, requiring cautious interpretation of non-significant $p$-values.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Identified Research Gaps}
Our review identifies three primary gaps in the current literature:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gap 1 (Lack of Standardized Ambiguity Metric Applications):} Most comparative trials evaluate gross requirement volume rather than directly quantifying ambiguity reduction across linguistic categories (lexical, syntactic, scope, vagueness).
    \item \textbf{Gap 2 (Absence of Empirical Longitudinal Rework Data):} While industrial frameworks indicate rework reductions from visual artifacts, longitudinal tracking of defect propagation across agile sprint deliveries remains scarce.
    \item \textbf{Gap 3 (Academic vs. Industrial Contextual Divide):} Rigorous controlled experiments are primarily conducted in university cohorts, whereas industrial investigations rely heavily on qualitative interviews.
\end{enumerate}
