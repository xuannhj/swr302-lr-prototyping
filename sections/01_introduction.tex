\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Requirements Engineering (RE) serves as the foundational cornerstone of the software development lifecycle. Flaws and omissions introduced during early requirements activities propagate exponentially downstream, resulting in severe budgetary overruns, architectural rework, and project abandonment \cite{pohl2016requirements, davis2018maturity}. Despite decades of methodological evolution, eliciting complete requirements and specifying them unambiguously remains one of the most stubborn challenges in software engineering practice.

\subsection{Problem statement and cognitive barriers}
A primary root cause of requirements defects resides in human cognitive and communication limitations. Empirical surveys demonstrate that over 70\% of software requirements are drafted in common natural language \cite{berry2003contract}. Natural language specifications are inherently prone to incompleteness and semantic ambiguity due to transformational linguistic effects, such as nominalization, universal quantifiers, and incompletely specified conditions~\cite{pohl2016requirements, berry2003contract}. Crucially, Berry et al. \cite{berry2003contract} identified the pervasive phenomenon of \textit{unconscious disambiguation}, wherein readers, oblivious to alternative meanings, instantaneously adopt their own intuitive interpretation and mistakenly assume it represents the sole consensus meaning. Furthermore, foundational RE frameworks, including the International Requirements Engineering Board (IREB) Certified Professional for Requirements Engineering (CPRE) curriculum \cite{pohl2016requirements}, emphasize that requirements elicitation is an act of mutual knowledge construction between heterogeneous stakeholders. Domain experts, end users, and engineers inhabit disparate conceptual worlds characterized by distinct professional vocabularies, mental models, and unstated assumptions. Stakeholders routinely take implicit background knowledge for granted, omitting critical domain logic from textual Software Requirements Specifications (SRS).

According to IREB Principle 4 (\textit{Adequate Change of Documentation Type}), prototyping mitigates these linguistic vulnerabilities by transforming abstract textual descriptions into concrete behavioral artifacts \cite{pohl2016requirements}. This transformation allows stakeholders to interact directly with the system, surfacing tacit assumptions and uncovering both subconscious baseline expectations (dissatisfiers) and unconscious value-adding needs (delighters) mapped across the Kano model. Furthermore, lengthy textual documents often cultivate an ``illusion of completeness,'' wherein passive approval of textual specifications masks critical omissions, lexical ambiguities, and divergent interpretations \cite{ferrari2016ambiguity, cascon2025ambiguity}. In modern agile settings, while concise user stories replace formal documents, Inayat et al. \cite{inayat2015systematic} revealed that minimal documentation frequently triggers acute communication gaps, unhandled quality attributes, and extensive sprint rework.

\subsection{The prototyping paradigm}
Prototyping offers a tangible, experiential mechanism to bridge this cognitive gap. Beyond serving as a superficial design preview, Lim, Stolterman, and Tenenberg \cite{lim2008anatomy} established the foundational concept of prototypes as \textit{filters} that traverse a design space and as \textit{manifestations} of conceptual ideas. Under Lim et al.'s formulation, the essential cognitive power of a prototype lies precisely in its \textit{incompleteness}: by purposefully filtering out extraneous architectural or aesthetic dimensions, a prototype channels stakeholder cognitive bandwidth directly onto targeted interaction mechanics and functional rules. By transforming abstract descriptions into concrete visual artifacts, prototyping establishes a shared mental model (\textit{shared cognitive artifact}) across technical and non-technical stakeholders \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}. Prototyping facilitates participative evaluation \cite{hickey1998prototyping}, stimulates conversational exploration, and grounds abstract requirements in contextual workflows.

Nevertheless, empirical questions persist regarding whether prototyping universally outperforms conventional elicitation and specification techniques, or whether its efficacy depends upon fidelity, interaction modalities, and specific requirement classifications.

\subsection{Terminology and conceptual foundations}
To establish conceptual clarity throughout this review, core constructs are operationalized following standard RE terminology. \textit{Missing requirements} denote functional rules, exceptional flows, boundary constraints, or system quality attributes that are necessary for fulfilling stakeholder goals but remain entirely omitted from specifications due to tacit knowledge lock-in or inadequate elicitation prompts. \textit{Unclear requirements} refer to requirement statements containing linguistic ambiguities (lexical, syntactic, scope) or non-verifiable subjective adjectives (vagueness), allowing multiple conflicting behavioral interpretations among stakeholders and developers \cite{cascon2025ambiguity, berry2003contract}. \textit{Prototyping modality and fidelity} encompass the medium and level of realism embodied in a prototype, spanning Low-Fidelity (Lo-Fi: paper cutouts, sketches, wireframes emphasizing functional flow without visual polish) and High-Fidelity (Hi-Fi: clickable Figma mockups, executable front-ends incorporating interactive behavior and realistic data). Finally, the \textit{Prototyping Assessment Model (PAM)} proposed by Bjarnason et al. \cite{bjarnason2023empirically} serves as the primary multi-dimensional taxonomy, classifying prototyping interventions across five interdependent facets: purpose, scope, media, exploration strategy, and use, anchored fundamentally in the economic principle of prototyping \cite{lim2008anatomy}.

\subsection{Research questions}
To systematically investigate this topic, this review is structured around three core research questions: \textit{RQ6.1} examines how prototypes are used to support requirements elicitation and validation; \textit{RQ6.2} evaluates whether using a prototype helps stakeholders identify missing or unclear requirements better than using textual specifications alone; and \textit{RQ6.3} investigates what types of requirements can be clarified more effectively through prototyping.

To enforce analytical rigor, primary studies are evaluated across three structured dimensions: (1) \textit{Rationale (The ``Why'')}, examining what cognitive barriers, tacit knowledge gaps, or textual specification failures necessitate prototyping; (2) \textit{Operational Protocol (The ``How'')}, detailing what empirical elicitation workflows, interaction protocols (e.g., Think-Aloud, Wizard-of-Oz), and validation sessions are executed; and (3) \textit{Taxonomical Classification (The ``What'')}, determining how prototypes are categorized across fidelity levels, media formats, and requirement types under models such as the PAM framework \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}.
