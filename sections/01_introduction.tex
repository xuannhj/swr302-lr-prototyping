\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Requirements Engineering (RE) is an essential phase in the software development lifecycle. Flaws and omissions introduced during early requirements activities propagate downstream, often resulting in budgetary overruns, architectural rework, and project delays \cite{pohl2016requirements, davis2018maturity}. Eliciting complete requirements and specifying them clearly remains a persistent challenge in software engineering practice.

\subsection{Problem statement and cognitive barriers}
A primary cause of requirements defects lies in communication and cognitive boundaries. Empirical surveys indicate that over 70\% of software requirements are written in natural language \cite{berry2003contract}. Natural language specifications are vulnerable to incompleteness and ambiguity due to linguistic effects such as nominalization, universal quantifiers, and underspecified conditions \cite{pohl2016requirements, berry2003contract}. As Berry et al. \cite{berry2003contract} observed, readers frequently experience \textit{unconscious disambiguation}, adopting an intuitive interpretation and assuming everyone shares the same understanding. Furthermore, foundational RE frameworks such as the International Requirements Engineering Board (IREB) curriculum \cite{pohl2016requirements} note that elicitation is a collaborative process among stakeholders with different professional backgrounds, vocabularies, and mental models. Stakeholders often take domain knowledge for granted, omitting critical logic from textual Software Requirements Specifications (SRS).

Under IREB Principle 4 (\textit{Adequate Change of Documentation Type}), prototyping helps address these communication gaps by translating abstract descriptions into interactive visual representations \cite{pohl2016requirements}. Direct interaction allows stakeholders to explore the system and reveal tacit assumptions, helping uncover both baseline expectations and unstated needs. Moreover, textual specifications can create a false sense of agreement, where passive sign-offs conceal omissions and differing interpretations \cite{ferrari2016ambiguity, cascon2025ambiguity}. In agile settings, while user stories offer brevity, Inayat et al. \cite{inayat2015systematic} showed that minimal documentation can lead to misunderstandings, overlooked quality attributes, and sprint rework.

\subsection{The prototyping paradigm}
Prototyping provides an interactive way to bridge this communication divide. Lim, Stolterman, and Tenenberg \cite{lim2008anatomy} describe prototypes as \textit{filters} that explore a design space and as \textit{manifestations} of design ideas. In their view, the strength of an early prototype lies in purposeful simplification: by leaving out unnecessary implementation details, prototypes help stakeholders focus on core workflows and business rules. In doing so, prototypes create a shared mental reference across technical and non-technical stakeholders \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}. Prototyping encourages participative evaluation \cite{hickey1998prototyping}, stimulates discussion, and grounds requirements in concrete user interactions.

However, questions remain regarding how prototyping compares empirically with traditional elicitation techniques, and how its effectiveness varies across prototype fidelity, interaction formats, and requirement types.

\subsection{Terminology and conceptual foundations}
To maintain consistency, key terms are defined following established RE literature. \textit{Missing requirements} refer to functional rules, exception flows, constraints, or quality attributes necessary for stakeholder goals that were omitted from specifications due to tacit assumptions or incomplete elicitation. \textit{Unclear requirements} denote statements containing linguistic ambiguities (lexical, syntactic, scope) or subjective adjectives (vagueness) that permit conflicting interpretations \cite{cascon2025ambiguity, berry2003contract}. \textit{Prototyping modality and fidelity} describe the medium and degree of realism, ranging from low-fidelity (paper sketches, wireframes focusing on functional flows) to high-fidelity (clickable mockups, interactive front-ends with realistic behavior). Finally, the \textit{Prototyping Assessment Model (PAM)} by Bjarnason et al. \cite{bjarnason2023empirically} provides a taxonomy classifying prototypes across five dimensions: purpose, scope, media, exploration strategy, and use, aligned with the economic principle of prototyping \cite{lim2008anatomy}.

\subsection{Research questions}
To investigate this topic systematically, this review addresses three research questions: \textit{RQ6.1} examines how prototypes support requirements elicitation and validation; \textit{RQ6.2} evaluates whether prototyping helps stakeholders identify missing or unclear requirements more effectively than textual specifications alone; and \textit{RQ6.3} examines what types of requirements are clarified most effectively through prototyping.

To analyze the literature, studies are examined across three dimensions: the rationale for using prototypes (addressing cognitive and linguistic gaps), the operational protocol (such as think-aloud sessions or validation workshops), and prototype characteristics (fidelity, media, and requirement types under the PAM model \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}).
