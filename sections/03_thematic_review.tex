\section{Thematic review}
\label{sec:thematic}

This section synthesizes findings addressing \textit{RQ6.1} (Operationalization of prototypes across elicitation and validation under the PAM framework) and \textit{RQ6.3} (Clarification efficacy categorized across requirement typologies).

\subsection{Prototyping operationalization and the PAM framework (RQ6.1)}
To conceptualize how prototypes are deployed in software engineering practice, Bjarnason, Lang, and Mj{\"o}berg \cite{bjarnason2023empirically} proposed the Prototyping Assessment Model (PAM), derived from a mapping study and an empirical investigation of 43 prototyping instances across 11 software organizations. The PAM taxonomy classifies prototyping across five dimensions: (1) \textit{purpose} (exploration, alignment, verification, testing fit); (2) \textit{scope} (horizontal workflow breadth vs. vertical feature depth); (3) \textit{media} (ranging from paper sketches to high-fidelity mockups); (4) \textit{use} (evaluators, testing formats, and evaluation contexts); and (5) \textit{exploration strategy} (single-track vs. parallel multi-alternative exploration).

This taxonomy is theoretically anchored in Lim, Stolterman, and Tenenberg's \cite{lim2008anatomy} concept of prototypes as \textit{filters} and \textit{manifestations}. Under Lim et al.'s formulation, the core efficacy of prototyping is governed by the \textit{economic principle of prototyping}: the best prototype is the simplest artifact that filters out irrelevant dimensions to answer targeted design questions. The PAM framework operationalizes this principle, enabling teams to omit unnecessary architectural details while focusing on user interaction and workflow feedback.

Empirical evidence from Bjarnason et al. \cite{bjarnason2023empirically} shows that prototypes serve as valuable cognitive anchors, especially in projects with minimal documentation. For instance, in a case study of Company J \cite{bjarnason2023empirically}, informal prototype walkthroughs (``corridor testing'') quickly revealed that the team had \textit{``missed several things,''} exposing functional omissions and misunderstandings that written specifications had obscured. Gupta et al. \cite{gupta2022strategic} observed a similar pattern in startups, showing that teams move from low-fidelity boundary artifacts during early exploration to interactive prototypes for feature refinement.

\subsection{Prototyping in elicitation versus validation}
The literature highlights distinct operational roles for prototypes across the RE lifecycle:

In \textit{elicitation mode (discovery)}, prototypes function as exploratory tools. Non-technical stakeholders often find it difficult to articulate tacit knowledge in abstract interviews \cite{ferrari2016ambiguity}; when presented with concrete mockups, unspoken business rules and missing steps surface as users interact with screens \cite{hickey1998prototyping}. Beyond human-led interviews, recent studies show that automated GUI prototyping can support self-elicitation of missing requirements. Kolthoff, J{\"a}hne, and Schneider \cite{kolthoff2024selfelicitation} developed SERGUI, an automated tool that generates GUI wireframes from initial task descriptions. By turning passive reading into interactive exploration, SERGUI helped non-technical stakeholders identify unstated requirements ($MAP = 0.741, MRR = 0.816$), showing that visual mockups can prompt users to recall edge cases and constraints even without a facilitator.

In \textit{validation mode (verification and evolution)}, prototypes serve as concrete verification artifacts. Rather than reading behavioral descriptions in an SRS, stakeholders evaluate actual interaction paths. Ferrari, Spoletini, and Debnath \cite{rej2022evolve} analyzed requirements evolution during prototype exploration, finding that requirements are co-created: only 30\%--38\% of documented user stories retained initial customer formulations, while hands-on prototype interaction led to the discovery of 42\% additional missing requirements and a 45\% increase in attention toward usability and quality constraints. Antonelli et al. \cite{antonelli2022iterative} similarly observed that prototype walkthroughs helped eliminate conflicting interpretations of textual use case scenarios.

\subsubsection{Functional requirements (FR)}
The literature shows broad consensus that prototyping is effective for capturing functional requirements. Visual screens allow users to trace step-by-step transaction flows, helping identify missing validation checks, unhandled edge cases, and omitted steps that text specifications often overlook \cite{rueda2020requirements}. This mechanism explains why Ferrari et al. \cite{rej2022evolve} found that 42\% of additional functional requirements were uncovered only when analysts and stakeholders interacted with concrete prototypes.

\subsubsection{Usability and user interface requirements}
Orme{\~n}o, Panach, and Pastor \cite{uv2023empirical} conducted an experiment with 48 participants evaluating the Usability Requirements Elicitation Method (UREM), which combines interactive paper mockups with usability guidelines. Their analysis showed that guiding paper mockup walkthroughs with structured guidelines produced a statistically significant increase in capturing usability requirements ($p = 0.000$, partial $\eta^2 = 0.274$) and guideline compliance ($p = 0.000$, partial $\eta^2 = 0.571$). Importantly, this approach resolved omission defects (such as input constraints and error notifications) without reducing analyst efficiency ($p = 0.220$). Similarly, Ferrari, Spoletini, and Debnath \cite{rej2022evolve} observed a 45\% increase in attention toward UI ergonomics during interactive sessions, while Pinheiro et al. \cite{jserd2019protopersona} showed that combining proto-personas with UI sketches helped non-technical stakeholders articulate subtle UX expectations.

\subsubsection{Non-functional requirements (NFR) and quality attributes}
Historically, low-fidelity paper prototypes struggled to capture non-functional requirements (such as performance, security, and reliability) \cite{rueda2020requirements, davis2018maturity}. Because paper cannot execute background operations or simulate latency, stakeholders naturally focus on visible interface elements and overlook quality attributes.

Researchers have addressed this limitation through three main approaches. Loud Paper Prototyping (LPP) \cite{abad2018loud} pairs paper mockups with Wizard-of-Oz simulation and a think-aloud protocol. In a three-year study of 50 mobile teams (183 participants), Abad et al. demonstrated that LPP significantly outperformed standard face-to-face requirements meetings in eliciting UI specifications ($p = 0.0001$) and usability/navigation NFRs ($p = 0.001$). Half of the teams explicitly captured NFRs, identifying navigation friction, layout issues, and omitted system states (such as offline modes and missing return paths).

Additionally, video-based prototypes help convey system context and operational constraints. Kopczy{\'n}ska and Shi \cite{bano2023videos} and Brill, Schneider, and Knauss \cite{brill2010videos} found that video scenarios realistically portray environmental noise and temporal pressure, helping stakeholders recognize latency, throughput, and safety requirements that static text rarely triggers.

Finally, in-situ prototyping in real operating environments addresses quality attributes in Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS). Hoffmann et al. \cite{hoffmann2011automotive} evaluated automotive infotainment prototypes embedded directly into vehicle cockpits (Audi A6 testbed) during live road navigation. While desktop and textual specifications missed environmental constraints, in-situ driver testing revealed strict visual distraction limits ($\le 1.5$ seconds), engine noise compensation for voice recognition, and telematics caching needs for mobile dead zones. These critical quality constraints only became apparent when the prototype was evaluated in its actual physical context.
